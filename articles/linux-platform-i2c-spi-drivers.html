Platform, I²C, and SPI Drivers: What Is Different?

Linux drivers share many patterns, but the bus model determines how devices are discovered, matched, and accessed. Platform, I²C, and SPI drivers are a useful starting comparison for embedded developers.

Platform devices

Platform devices commonly represent SoC-integrated peripherals such as GPIO controllers, timers, or memory-mapped blocks. Their resources are often described by Device Tree: register ranges, interrupts, clocks, and resets. Drivers use the platform driver model and typically access registers through managed mapping helpers.

I²C devices

I²C devices live behind an I²C controller and are addressed on the bus. A driver uses the I²C client model and SMBus or I²C transfer helpers. The reg property usually represents the device's bus address, not a memory-mapped register base. Register reads often involve a write of a register index followed by a repeated-start read, depending on the device protocol.

SPI devices

SPI devices are selected through chip-select and communicate through full-duplex transfers. A driver uses the SPI framework and message/transfer APIs. Clock mode, maximum frequency, word size, and chip-select timing must match the peripheral datasheet.

Common mistake

Do not choose a driver model based only on the sensor's electrical pins. A sensor connected over I²C should normally use the I²C framework, even if it also has an interrupt GPIO. The GPIO signals an event; it does not change the data bus.

Bring-up sequence

  • Verify wiring, power, pin mux, and controller enablement.
  • Describe the device correctly in firmware or board data.
  • Use the right subsystem APIs and matching table.
  • Check bus-level traces and kernel logs before blaming the sensor.

Final takeaway

Low-level engineering is about understanding the contract between hardware, firmware, and the operating system. Start with the specification, form a testable hypothesis, collect evidence, and validate behavior on the target platform.
